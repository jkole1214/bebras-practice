J-A4 Irrigation System (10207) interactive gates. Canal network from a lake down to 6 fields; ungated forks send water both ways, four binary gates send water only left or only right. Target: irrigate exactly a given subset (2, 4, 5, 6). Work from the fields that must stay dry: field 3 is fed only by a central channel reachable from two gates, so both must point away from it; that forces the left gate, which then forces the lower-left gate away from field 1; the right side's ungated fork already feeds 5 and 6, and the last gate must point to 4. Solution: upper-left gate left, lower-left gate right, upper-right gate right, lower-right gate left. Verified. Difference from Benjamin's Beaver Dam: gates are 2-way switches (always send water somewhere) rather than open/closed, and ungated forks plus merging channels make it a DAG, not a tree. Concepts: reachability in a DAG, constraint propagation from forbidden targets, boolean switch settings (4 gates = 16 configurations, small enough to brute force but the intended path is propagation).

J-B4 Drawing Stars (432) MC labels n:k. Star polygons labeled by dot count n and step k (connect each dot to its k-th nearest neighbor around the circle). Worked examples cover a single-loop star, a star made of two separate triangles, a plain polygon (k=1), and a k=3 star. Count the 10 dots, then from one dot count how many positions its line skips: step 4. Answer 10:4 (verified). Distractors: miscounting dots (9) or off-by-one step (3, 5). Concepts: modular arithmetic (visiting i, i+k, i+2k mod n), notation as an encoding of a structure, gcd(n,k) decides one loop vs several (6:2 is two triangles because gcd is 2). Counting a step on a crowded drawing is the error-prone part.

J-C1 Popularity (10214) MC 7 names. Friendship graph of 7 people. Each person posts their own photo on every friend's page; anyone can view their own page and friends' pages. A clean generator family: random small graphs where the max-degree node is not the max 2-hop node.

J-C2 Word Chains (10213) MC labeled pictures. Six 3-letter words joined when they differ in one letter (a word-ladder graph: two degree-3 hubs joined to each other, a triangle through one hub, a 2-step tail off one hub, a 1-step leaf off the other). The labels are erased and the graph is redrawn in a different layout; pick the labeling that preserves every edge. Solve by invariants: find the triangle (fixes the third vertex), then tell the two hubs apart by what hangs off them (a single leaf vs a 2-node tail). Answer: the option with the leaf hub at top (verified). Distractors place hubs or the tail wrongly. Concepts: graph isomorphism with labels (harder than Benjamin's Walnut Animals because you must produce the mapping, not just detect it), degree and structural invariants, Hamming-distance-1 graphs.

J-C3 Hard Fireworks (423) numeric entry. A variable-length code over two symbols (5 words, codewords of length 1 to 3) that is NOT prefix-free, so a received 8-symbol signal can be split into words in several ways. Count the distinct parses. Systematic method: dynamic programming left to right (ways[i] = sum of ways[i - len(c)] for each codeword c ending at i), or a tree of choices. Answer: 4 (verified by enumeration). Errors: stopping at the first parse found; missing parses where a short word is used; double-counting the same word sequence reached two ways. Concepts: ambiguous codes and why real codes are prefix-free (Huffman, Morse needs gaps), counting by recursion/DP, string segmentation. Strong generator family: random codebooks plus a signal, answer computed by DP; distractors from greedy-parse-only (1), off-by-one enumeration.

J-C4 Pirate Hunters (10212) MC (2, 3, 5 turns, or never). Pursuit game on a 7-node ring: two chasers move one step to a free neighbor; the runner jumps exactly two steps; the runner is caught only when both of its jump targets hold a chaser. Chasers move first. The start position already has the chasers on both of the runner's jump targets, but the chasers are obliged to move, which breaks the trap, and the runner can always restore an escape. Answer: the police can never force a capture (verified; also confirmed by exhaustive game search, no forced win at any depth). Trap: the interactive board invites trial play, and a student who plays a few games and catches a careless pirate picks 2 or 3. The real question is about optimal play by both sides. Concepts: game trees and minimax, "for all opponent replies" quantifier, invariants that prove impossibility, zugzwang (being forced to move is a disadvantage). This is the first example task whose correct answer is "impossible".

J-C5 Bowl Factory (10211) numeric entry. Each worker performs one right-to-left adjacent-swap pass over a 6-item list (a single bubble-sort pass, which carries the smallest remaining item all the way to the front). Worked example shows one pass. Question: how many workers (passes) sort a given permutation. Simulate passes, or reason: each pass moves every out-of-place small item at most... the key fact is that one pass fully places the minimum of the unsorted part, while larger items move right by at most one place per pass, so the count is set by the item that must travel furthest right one step at a time. For the given order the answer is 4 (verified; simulated pass by pass). Errors: assuming a pass fully sorts, counting swaps instead of passes, simulating left-to-right instead of the direction shown. Concepts: bubble sort, passes vs swaps, asymmetric movement of elements in one pass, worst-case counting.

S-B2 Beaver the Alchemist (10217) MC 5/10/11/12. Recipe DAG: a base item combines into intermediate items, which combine into a final item; every conversion consumes its inputs. S-C1 Spies (10222) numeric entry. Parallel gossip: each round is a matching (every person meets at most one other), and a meeting merges both people's knowledge. A worked example shows 6 people finishing in 3 rounds. Question: minimum rounds for 5 people. With an odd count someone sits out every round, which costs an extra round: answer 4 (verified; exhaustive search over all matchings confirms 3 is impossible). Errors: answering 3 by analogy with the example (fewer people must be no slower), or 5 by assuming one new piece per round. Concepts: information spreading / broadcast in parallel, doubling (knowledge at most doubles per round, so at least ceil(log2 n) rounds), parity obstruction for odd n, proving a lower bound vs exhibiting a schedule. The counterintuitive result (fewer people, more rounds) is the point of the task.

S-C2 Building a Chip (10223) MC 5/13/15/16. Grid of contacts (6 rows x 9 columns) with some contacts already used; route a wire from S to R with horizontal/vertical unit steps that avoid used contacts. Count the shortest routes. Method: BFS layering from S, writing at each contact the number of shortest ways to reach it (sum of the counts of its neighbors one layer closer), the same Pascal's-triangle trick as counting lattice paths, with obstacles. Shortest length is 13 steps; there are 15 shortest routes (verified; BFS count confirms). Distractor 13 is the length, not the count (the classic confusion of "how long" with "how many"). Concepts: shortest paths in a grid graph, counting paths by DP over BFS layers, obstacles, combinatorics of lattice paths.

S-C4 Kangaroo (10221) MC 10 letters. A jumper on 10 plates in a row starts at the left end and may only jump +2 or -3, never landing on a visited plate. If it visits all 10, which plate is last? Search the tree of jump sequences with backtracking: from the left end the first moves are forced (+2, +2), then most branches dead-end quickly. Exactly one complete tour exists (A C E B D F H J G I), so the last plate is the 9th, I (verified; exhaustive search confirms uniqueness). Errors: assuming the tour ends at the far right end (J); stopping at a dead end and guessing. Concepts: Hamiltonian path, backtracking search, pruning dead ends early, uniqueness as a hint that a forced chain exists.

S-C5 Mobiles (10224) MC 4 pictures. A nested bracket notation describes a hanging mobile: each bracket is (position child), where position is a signed offset on the parent stick and child is either a count of figures hung in a chain or another stick described the same way. A worked example pairs a short expression with its picture. Given a 3-level expression, pick the matching picture. Parse recursively: top stick has two sub-sticks; the left one carries a 4-figure chain and a small stick with one figure at each end; the right one carries a 6-figure chain and a 3-figure chain. Answer: the fourth picture (verified). Distractors swap which sub-stick holds the nested stick, or change a chain length by one. Concepts: recursive data structures, parsing nested brackets (S-expressions), tree serialization, mapping text to structure. Senior equivalent of Benjamin's Beaver Lunch schema task, but the student must build the tree from text.

B22-A1 Lila's Guessing Game (24139) drag items into bags. Three containers each hold one item; a sequence of three pairwise swaps is applied; place each item where it ends. Trace state after each swap (it is a permutation composed of three transpositions). Answer: the item that started in the third bag ends in the first, the second bag's item ends where it started, the first bag's item ends in the third (verified). Errors: applying swaps in the wrong order, or applying each swap to the original positions instead of the current state. Concepts: STATE, SEQ_EXEC, SWAP_TEMP (variable swap as data movement). UI note: drop slots reject an item if another sits there mid-drag; place items in an order that never needs an occupied slot.

B22-A2 Tortoise and Hare (24140) click a cell. A track is a lead-in path of 4 cells that feeds into a loop of 6 cells (a "rho" shape). Two runners start together; one moves 1 cell per minute, the other 2. Find the first cell where they meet after the start. Simulate minute by minute, or reason: they meet when the gap between them is a multiple of the loop length while both are on the loop. They meet after 6 minutes, on the loop cell 6 steps from the start (the second cell of the upper loop) (verified). Errors: assuming they meet at the loop's entrance; forgetting the lead-in; moving the fast runner 2 steps but checking positions only every other minute. Concepts: SIMULATION, MODULAR_ARITHMETIC, cycle detection (this is Floyd's tortoise-and-hare algorithm in miniature).

B22-A3 Colorful Tower (24141) click tiles to color. A triangle of hexagonal tiles: the bottom row is given, and every tile rests on two tiles below. Rule: a tile and the two beneath it are either all the same color or all different. So each tile is forced: same color if the two below match, otherwise the third color. Fill row by row upward (15 tiles). Verified correct. Errors: applying the rule to a tile and its side neighbors instead of the two below; mixing up "all different" when the two below already match. Concepts: CONSTRAINT_SAT with a forced, deterministic propagation, a rule applied repeatedly (cellular-automaton style; the same as addition mod 3). Strong generator family: random bottom rows, answer computed by the rule.

B22-A4 Most Popular Book (24138) click one of 4 books. A borrowing log lists (reader symbol, book picture) pairs; count which book appears most. Answer: the cat book, 3 of 7 entries (verified). The reader column is a distractor: it does not affect the count. Concepts: tallying, reading a table, ignoring irrelevant data. Very easy at Benjamin tier A.

B22-A5 Beehive (24142) drag 7 bees into a 7-cell hive. Each bee's rule is a small cell pattern with the bee's cell marked; the bee may only sit in a cell where that whole pattern fits inside the hive. For each bee list the cells where its pattern fits; start with the bees that fit in only one cell (three of them), remove those cells, and the rest are forced in turn. Verified correct.
